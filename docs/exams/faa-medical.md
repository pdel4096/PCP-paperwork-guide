# FAA medical exams for pilots

When a patient says "I need my flight physical," they mean one of two very different things, and it is worth sorting that out in the first thirty seconds of the visit.

The first is an **FAA medical certificate** — first, second, or third class — issued by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) after an exam by an **Aviation Medical Examiner (AME)**. You cannot do this one. Same story as the commercial driver physical: only a physician who holds a current FAA designation as an AME can perform the exam, and an exam by anyone else produces nothing. Send them to the [FAA's AME locator](https://www.faa.gov/pilots/amelocator/). Becoming an AME requires FAA training and designation, and it is a reasonable thing to pursue if you have a lot of pilots in your panel.

The second is **BasicMed**, and this one lands squarely on your desk, because the exam may be performed by *any state-licensed physician*. Most primary care physicians (PCPs) have never heard of it until a patient hands them the form.

## BasicMed: the one you can actually do

BasicMed is the alternative to a third-class medical certificate that Congress created in 2016 and expanded in the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024. A pilot flying under BasicMed is not certified by the FAA at all. Instead they hold a driver's license, get a physical from a state-licensed physician every 48 months, take a free online aeromedical course every 24 months, and keep both documents in their logbook.

**Nothing is submitted to the FAA.** The completed checklist goes in the pilot's logbook and is produced on request. This surprises people — it surprised me — and it changes the character of what you are signing.

To be eligible, the pilot must hold a valid U.S. driver's license and must have held an FAA medical certificate at some point after July 14, 2006, that was not subsequently denied, suspended, revoked, or withdrawn. If they have never held one, BasicMed is not available and they need an AME.

### The form

It is [FAA Form 8700-2, the Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist (CMEC)](https://www.faa.gov/forms/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentID/1030366). Three sections: instructions, a health history the pilot completes, and an exam section you complete. It is a plain physical — vitals, general appearance, head and neck, heart, lungs, abdomen, extremities, neurologic, vision, hearing, mental status. Nothing exotic. No labs, no electrocardiogram, no urine dip.

One quirk worth knowing: the printed form still carries the *old* aircraft limits (six occupants, 6,000 pounds). The current statutory limits are seven occupants and 12,500 pounds, which is what the [FAA's BasicMed page](https://www.faa.gov/licenses_certificates/airmen_certification/basic_med) says. The form is stale; the FAA page is right. Do not let a pilot talk you into "correcting" the form.

### What you are actually signing

You sign a declaration that you performed the exam and that you are not aware of any medical condition that, as presently treated, could interfere with the person's ability to operate an aircraft safely. Read that twice. It is a statement about your awareness following an examination — not a certification of airworthiness, and not a promise that the pilot is safe. You are also signing a federal document, with the usual 18 U.S.C. § 1001 penalties printed on it for knowingly false statements.

My practice, and my recommendation:

- **Do it as a real visit, not a hallway favor.** Schedule it. Do the whole checklist. Document that you did.
- **Review the medication list line by line** and write down that you did. This is the single item most likely to matter later.
- **Do not sign it for a patient you don't know.** If they are established with you, fine. If they walked in cold with a form, you have no history to be "aware" of, which is exactly the position you do not want to be in.
- **Bill it as a non-covered service.** Like a commercial driver physical or a camp form, it is a regulatory exam, not a covered preventive visit. USe an office visit code and ICD-10 code like "Encounter for Administrative Physical"
- **Give the original back to the patient** and keep a copy in the chart. You are not their record-keeper for this.

If you are not comfortable signing, don't. There is no obligation, and a pilot who wants a physician who knows aviation medicine can pay an AME to do the BasicMed exam instead.

### Conditions that require the FAA first

Some medical conditions **require** initial evaluation by the FAA through an AME before they can use BasicMed. These include:

- **Mental health:** severe personality disorder with overt acts, psychosis, bipolar disorder, or substance dependence within the previous two years
- **Neurologic:** epilepsy, disturbance of consciousness without satisfactory explanation, or transient loss of nervous system control without satisfactory explanation
- **Cardiovascular:** myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease that has required treatment, cardiac valve replacement, or heart replacement (one-time special issuance per diagnosis)

If one of these turns up in your history-taking, stop and send them to an AME. Signing the checklist does not make the problem go away, and the pilot is the one who eats the consequences.

## The part that matters more than the form: the medication list

Most of the harm a PCP does in aviation medicine has nothing to do with paperwork. It is writing a prescription that grounds someone, without either of you realizing it.

The FAA maintains a [Do Not Issue / Do Not Fly list](https://www.faa.gov/ame_guide/media/DNI_DNF_tables.pdf) and a broader [pharmaceuticals section in the Guide for Aviation Medical Examiners](https://www.faa.gov/ame_guide/pharm). The headlines:

- **Stimulants for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder are categorically disqualifying** — methylphenidate, amphetamine salts, lisdexamfetamine — as are the common non-stimulant agents used for the same indication. This is the single most consequential prescription you can write for a young patient with aviation ambitions, and the diagnosis itself follows them forever on the application.
- **Antidepressants** now run through what the FAA renamed the **Antidepressant Protocol** (it was the SSRI Protocol). Conditionally acceptable agents, single-agent only, are listed in the FAA's [antidepressant medications table](https://www.faa.gov/ame_guide/media/Antidepressant_Medications.pdf); the four original selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors were joined by sustained- or extended-release bupropion in 2023 and by duloxetine, venlafaxine, and desvenlafaxine in 2024. Anything off that list has no special issuance pathway at all. If you are starting an antidepressant in a pilot, picking one from the list costs you nothing and can save them a year.
- **Benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines, muscle relaxants, opioids, sleep aids, and anything labeled for drowsiness** are Do Not Fly, with a wait time after the last dose of five times the maximum half-life (or five times the maximum dosing interval if half-life is unknown).
- **Anything scheduled, and any open-ended prescription for chronic use of a controlled substance**, is Do Not Issue.

The practical rule: before you start a new medication in a pilot, look it up, or tell them to call their AME before the first dose. Choosing a certifiable drug on the front end is trivial. Unwinding a certification problem on the back end takes months and thousands of dollars.

## Records requests and letters

An AME who defers an application, or the FAA's Aerospace Medical Certification Division, will often ask the pilot to obtain records from you. As always, you release nothing without the patient's written authorization on your own release form.

What the FAA wants is boring and specific. A useful letter states:

- The diagnosis and the date it was made
- What workup established it, with copies of the actual reports
- Current treatment, including dose and how long they have been on it
- Whether the condition is stable, and on what evidence
- Any side effects, or an explicit statement that there are none
- Prognosis and follow-up interval

What the FAA does not want is your opinion on whether they should be certified. That is not your call, and volunteering it does not help. Attach the records; the reviewers in Oklahoma City would rather read a lab report than a paragraph of reassurance.

> **Sample framing**
>
> Mr. Patient has been under my care since [date]. He carries a diagnosis of [condition], established [date] on the basis of [studies, attached]. He has been treated with [drug, dose] since [date]. His condition has been clinically stable for [duration], as documented by [objective findings]. He reports no side effects from treatment. I see him every [interval]; his next appointment is [date]. Records from [date range] are enclosed. Please contact me if additional information would be helpful.

## Honesty, and the request you will eventually get

Sooner or later a pilot will ask you to leave something out of the chart, or to change a diagnosis, or to not send a particular note. The answer is no. The application is a federal document and the pilot signs it under penalty of prosecution; you are not doing them a kindness by helping them build a case for falsification.

The obligation runs the other way too. **Be accurate in both directions.** A sloppy diagnosis code dropped into a chart to justify a visit — "depression" on a screening encounter, "syncope" for a vasovagal episode at a blood draw — can cost a pilot a year and several thousand dollars to unwind. Code what the patient actually has.

## When you don't think they should be flying

You have no duty to report a pilot to the FAA, and there is no aviation equivalent of the state medical review board referral you would use for a driver.

What you do have is [14 CFR § 61.53](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/61.53), which prohibits a pilot from flying while they know or have reason to know of a condition that would disqualify them. That obligation is theirs, and most pilots take it seriously if you tell them plainly that it exists. Say it out loud, say what specifically concerns you, and write down that you said it. If you remain worried after that, it is a risk management question, not a medical one — call your own risk management or legal department before you contact anyone at the FAA.

## Quick reference: certificate classes and duration

| Class | Used for | Under 40 at exam | Age 40+ at exam |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Airline transport pilot | 12 calendar months | 6 calendar months |
| Second | Commercial operations | 12 calendar months | 12 calendar months |
| Third | Private, recreational, student | 60 calendar months | 24 calendar months |

Certificates expire on the last day of the month, and they step down rather than dying outright — a first-class certificate keeps working at second-class and then third-class privileges for the longer intervals above. Age is fixed on the date of the exam.

**Sport pilots** are a separate case: they fly on a valid U.S. driver's license with no medical certificate at all. The MOSAIC rule that took effect in October 2025 substantially widened the range of aircraft that covers, so you may hear this from more patients than you used to.
